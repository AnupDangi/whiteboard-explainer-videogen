# Spaced repetition — V1 benchmark source (dev set)

## Forgetting

New memories fade fast. Without review, most of a lesson is gone within a
day. The decline is steepest in the first hours and then levels off. This
pattern is called the forgetting curve.

## The spacing effect

Reviews scheduled at increasing intervals slow forgetting far more than the
same reviews crammed together. Spreading practice over days beats massing
it in one evening, even with equal total study time.

## How intervals grow

A practical schedule reviews new material after one day, then after three
days, then after a week, then after a month. Each successful recall earns a
longer wait; each failure shortens it. The intervals expand because every
recall strengthens the memory trace.

## Active recall

Rereading feels productive but barely helps. Testing yourself — closing the
book and reproducing the fact — forces retrieval, and retrieval is what
builds durable memory. Every spaced repetition should be a recall attempt,
not a reread.

## Why cramming fails

Cramming loads facts into short-term memory, which feels like learning and
passes tomorrow's quiz. But without spaced retrieval the traces decay, and
weeks later almost nothing remains. Spacing trades a little extra effort
now for months of retention.

## Interleaving

Mixing topics within a session — alternating vocabulary, grammar, and
listening — adds a second spacing benefit: each topic's repetitions spread
further apart, and the brain practices choosing the right method, not just
executing one.

## A minimal routine

Learn a small set of items. Test yourself after ten minutes, the next day,
three days later, and weekly after that. Retire items recalled instantly
three times in a row; restart the schedule for anything missed.
