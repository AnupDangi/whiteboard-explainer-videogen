# Osmosis — V1 benchmark source (dev set)

## Definition

Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules across a selectively permeable
membrane, from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water
potential. It requires no energy input from the cell and is therefore a form
of passive transport.

## The membrane

A selectively permeable membrane allows water molecules to pass freely while
restricting the movement of most solute particles, such as salts and sugars.
The cell membrane of animal cells and the cell membrane plus cell wall of
plant cells behave this way.

## Direction of movement

Water always moves down its water potential gradient. Pure water has the
highest possible water potential. Dissolving a solute in water lowers its
water potential, so water moves toward the more concentrated solution. When
solute concentrations are equal on both sides, there is no net movement,
although individual molecules continue to cross in both directions.

## Osmotic pressure

If osmosis is allowed to proceed in a confined compartment, the inflow of
water raises the hydrostatic pressure. The pressure required to exactly stop
the net inflow is called the osmotic pressure. It increases with the
difference in solute concentration across the membrane.

## Effects on cells

Placed in pure water, an animal cell such as a red blood cell swells as water
enters, and may burst. This bursting is called lysis. Plant cells resist
bursting because the rigid cell wall pushes back; the resulting internal
pressure is turgor pressure, which keeps stems and leaves firm.

Placed in a concentrated salt solution, cells lose water and shrink. In
plants this shrinking of the cell contents away from the wall is called
plasmolysis, and the plant wilts.

## Uptake in roots

Root hair cells absorb water from the soil because the cell sap inside the
root hair has a lower water potential than the soil water around it. The
large surface area of root hairs increases the rate of uptake.

## Factors affecting the rate

A larger membrane surface area, a steeper water potential gradient, and a
higher temperature all increase the rate of osmosis. Membrane thickness and
the size of the solute particles also matter: only water and very small
molecules cross freely.
