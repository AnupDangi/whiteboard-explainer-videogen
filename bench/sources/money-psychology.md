# Why people overspend — V1 video source (psychology of money)

## Pain of paying

Parting with money registers as mild pain in the brain. Cash hurts most
because the loss is visible and immediate. Cards hurt less, and one-click
buying hurts least of all — the same purchase feels cheaper when the
payment is invisible.

## Present bias

People overweight today and underweight next year. A small reward now beats
a larger reward later in most snap decisions. This present bias explains
impulse carts, skipped savings, and diets broken by dessert.

## Mental accounts

Households keep informal mental budgets: rent money, fun money, gift
money. A dollar is a dollar, yet people treat a tax refund as bonus cash
while guarding salary closely. Labels change behavior without changing
the math.

## Anchors and sales

The first number seen becomes a reference point. A crossed-out price of
one hundred makes sixty feel like a bargain even if the item is worth
forty. Sellers set anchors deliberately; buyers can set their own by
checking a second source first.

## Loss aversion

Losses sting roughly twice as much as equal gains please. This asymmetry
makes people hold losing investments too long and sell winners too early.
Naming the feeling — "I am avoiding a realized loss" — weakens its grip.

## Friction as a tool

Since invisible payments overspend, adding small friction helps: deleting
saved cards, waiting a day before big purchases, and moving savings
automatically on payday. Design the environment so the easy choice is the
wise one.
