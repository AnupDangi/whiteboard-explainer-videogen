# Stress and coping in midlife — V1 video source (adult psychology)

## The midlife load

Middle adulthood often stacks career peaks, teenage children, and aging
parents at the same time. Psychologists call this combination role
overload: more real demands than hours or energy allow.

## Acute versus chronic stress

Acute stress is a short alarm — a deadline, a near accident. The body
releases adrenaline, focuses attention, then recovers. Chronic stress is
different: months of pressure without full recovery, which wears down
sleep, immunity, and mood.

## Problem-focused coping

When a stressor can change, direct action works best: renegotiating a
deadline, arranging elder care, or setting a boundary. Writing the problem
down and listing one small next step converts vague dread into a task.

## Emotion-focused coping

Some stressors cannot change — a diagnosis, a loss. Here healthy coping
means handling the feeling: talking with trusted people, brief daily
walks, regular sleep, and limiting alcohol. Avoidance through overwork or
drinking raises the long-term cost.

## Social support

Perceived support predicts resilience better than received help. Knowing
someone would listen if asked calms the stress response even before any
help arrives. Maintaining two or three close ties is protective.

## Recovery rituals

Short daily recovery — an evening walk, ten quiet minutes, a kept
bedtime — lets the nervous system complete its stress cycle. Without
recovery, each day starts from yesterday's leftover tension.
