# Half-life — V1 benchmark source (dev set)

## Unstable atoms

Some atomic nuclei are unstable: they spontaneously transform into a
different nucleus by emitting radiation. A single atom's moment of decay is
completely random and unpredictable.

## From atoms to samples

Although one atom is unpredictable, a large sample behaves with clockwork
regularity. Out of millions of atoms, a fixed fraction decays every second.
This fraction per unit time is the decay constant of the material.

## Definition

The half-life of a substance is the time required for exactly half of the
atoms in a sample to decay. After one half-life, one half remains. After a
second half-life, half of that half remains — one quarter of the original.

## The decay curve

Plotting the remaining atoms against time gives a curve that falls steeply
at first and then flattens, approaching zero without ever quite reaching
it. Each equal time interval removes the same fraction, not the same
number, of what is left.

## Examples

Different substances have wildly different half-lives. Iodine-131, used in
medicine, has a half-life of about 8 days. Carbon-14, used to date ancient
remains, has a half-life of about 5,730 years. Uranium-238 has a half-life
of about 4.5 billion years — nearly the age of the Earth.

## Why half remains, not all

A common confusion is that after two half-lives nothing remains. In fact
each half-life acts only on what survived the previous one: after two
half-lives one quarter remains, after three one eighth, and so on through
repeated halving.

## Uses

Doctors choose short half-life tracers so radiation fades quickly from the
patient. Archaeologists measure the remaining carbon-14 in wood or bone and
read the age off the decay curve. Engineers design nuclear waste stores
around the longest half-lives in the waste.
