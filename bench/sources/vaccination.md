# Vaccination and immunity — V1 benchmark source (dev set)

## The threat

Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens such as viruses and bacteria.
When a pathogen enters the body in sufficient numbers, it multiplies and
causes illness.

## First encounter

The immune system needs time to recognize a new pathogen. Special white
blood cells must first identify it, then manufacture matching antibodies —
proteins that tag the pathogen for destruction. This first response is slow,
taking days to weeks, which is why a first infection usually makes a person
sick.

## Memory cells

After the infection clears, the immune system keeps memory cells: long-lived
cells that remember the pathogen's signature. Memory cells persist for years,
sometimes for life.

## Second encounter

If the same pathogen returns, memory cells recognize it immediately and
launch a fast, strong antibody response within hours. The pathogen is
destroyed before it can cause illness. This protection is called immunity.

## What a vaccine does

A vaccine introduces a harmless version of the pathogen — a weakened germ,
an inactivated germ, or just a fragment such as a surface protein. It cannot
cause the disease, but it carries the signature the immune system learns.
The body runs a practice response and keeps memory cells, exactly as after
a real infection but without the illness.

## Why two doses

The first dose primes the system and creates a small memory pool. The
second dose, given weeks later, expands that pool dramatically. Booster
doses later in life refresh memory that fades with time.

## Community protection

When enough people are immune, a pathogen cannot find new hosts and its
spread stalls. This indirect protection of the few who cannot be
vaccinated is called herd immunity.
