# How children learn language — V1 video source (child psychology)

## Imitation first

Long before they speak in sentences, children imitate the sounds around
them. Babbling practices the mouth muscles, and adults instinctively reply
with exaggerated melody — called parentese — which holds the baby's
attention and marks word boundaries.

## One word carries a sentence

Around the first birthday, a single word can mean a whole thought. A child
who says "milk" may mean "I want milk" or "the milk spilled". Adults infer
the meaning from context, gesture, and eye gaze.

## Two-word combinations

Near age two, children combine words: "more juice", "daddy go". These
mini-sentences follow the child's home language order even though no one
teaches grammar rules explicitly. The pattern emerges from listening.

## Overregularization

Preschoolers apply rules too broadly and say things like "goed" or
"mouses". These charming errors prove the child learned the rule itself —
memorized words would never produce them. Gentle exposure to correct forms,
not correction drills, guides the pattern into place.

## Conversation grows vocabulary

The number of back-and-forth exchanges predicts vocabulary growth better
than hours of background television. Each conversational turn gives the
child a reason to listen, interpret, and respond.

## Reading aloud

Daily read-aloud time exposes children to rare words and complex sentences
missing from everyday speech. Pointing at pictures while naming them links
new sounds to visible meaning, the foundation of later reading.
