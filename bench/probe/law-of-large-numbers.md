# The law of large numbers

When you flip a fair coin, each flip has a probability of one half of landing heads. Yet in a short run of flips the results can look far from half. Ten flips might give seven heads, which is seventy percent.

The law of large numbers says that as the number of trials grows, the average of the results gets closer to the expected value. After a thousand flips, the fraction of heads is usually very close to one half. The more flips, the smaller the typical gap between the observed fraction and the true probability.

This law does not say that the past flips are corrected by future flips. A common mistake, called the gambler's fallacy, is to think that after a run of tails a head is due. Each flip is independent, so the next flip is still fifty-fifty. The fraction moves toward one half because new flips dilute the early imbalance, not because the coin remembers.

Pollsters and scientists rely on this idea. A small sample can be misleading, but a larger random sample gives an average that is closer to the average of the whole population. The sample size matters more than the percentage of the population sampled.

The law has a cost. To cut the typical error in half, you need about four times as many observations. Gathering more data always helps, but each extra improvement becomes more expensive.
