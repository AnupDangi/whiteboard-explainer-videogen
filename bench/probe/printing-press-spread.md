# Why the printing press spread ideas so quickly

Before the 1450s, books in Europe were copied by hand. A scribe needed months to copy one book, so books were rare and expensive, and only monasteries, universities, and wealthy people owned them.

Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg built a printing press that used movable metal type. Each letter was cast as a separate piece of metal. Printers arranged the pieces into a page, inked them, and pressed paper against them. After printing, the pieces could be rearranged and reused for the next page.

Movable type changed the cost of a book. One press could produce hundreds of identical copies in the time a scribe copied a single book. As the number of copies rose, the price of each copy fell. Cheaper books meant more people could afford to buy them.

More readers created demand for more books, and printers set up shops in cities across Europe. By 1500 there were presses in more than two hundred towns. Printed books also looked the same from copy to copy, so scholars in different cities could refer to the same page numbers and compare their work.

Ideas therefore travelled faster. In the early 1500s, the writings of Martin Luther were printed and sold in large numbers within a few years, helping the Reformation spread across Europe. Printing also supported the growth of science, because researchers could publish results that others could check and build on. The press did not create new ideas by itself; it lowered the cost of copying and sharing them.
