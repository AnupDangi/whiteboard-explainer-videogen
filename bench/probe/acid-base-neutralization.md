# Acid–base neutralization

An acid is a substance that releases hydrogen ions, written H+, when it dissolves in water. A base is a substance that releases hydroxide ions, written OH-. The pH scale measures how acidic a solution is. A pH below 7 is acidic, a pH of 7 is neutral, and a pH above 7 is basic.

When an acid and a base are mixed, the hydrogen ions from the acid combine with the hydroxide ions from the base to form water. This reaction is called neutralization. The other ions stay dissolved and form a salt. For example, hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to give water and sodium chloride, which is table salt.

The amount of acid and base matters. If the acid and the base supply exactly equal numbers of H+ and OH- ions, every ion is used up and the solution becomes neutral, with a pH close to 7. If there is more acid than base, some H+ ions remain and the solution stays acidic. If there is more base than acid, some OH- ions remain and the solution stays basic.

Chemists use this idea in a titration. They add a base of known concentration to an acid drop by drop. An indicator changes color when the pH passes through 7, marking the endpoint where the acid has just been neutralized. From the volume of base used, they calculate the concentration of the acid.

Neutralization also releases heat, so the mixture warms slightly as the reaction proceeds.
