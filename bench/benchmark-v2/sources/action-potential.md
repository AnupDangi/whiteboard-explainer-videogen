# The neuronal action potential

A neuron at rest keeps its inside about 70 millivolts more negative than its outside. This resting membrane potential is about -70 mV. It is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in using ATP, and by leak channels that let potassium out.

An action potential begins when a stimulus depolarizes the membrane to the threshold, about -55 mV. Voltage-gated sodium channels open, and sodium ions rush in down their concentration gradient. This inward current depolarizes the membrane further, which opens more sodium channels. This positive feedback drives the potential up to about +40 mV.

At the peak, sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium ions flow out, and the membrane repolarizes. The potassium channels close slowly, so the potential briefly overshoots below rest. This is hyperpolarization. The sodium-potassium pump and leak channels then restore the resting potential.

While sodium channels are inactivated, the neuron cannot fire again. This is the absolute refractory period. It makes the signal travel in one direction along the axon. In myelinated axons the action potential jumps between gaps in the myelin called nodes of Ranvier, a process called saltatory conduction, which speeds up the signal.
