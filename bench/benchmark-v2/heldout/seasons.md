# Why Earth has seasons

Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's rotation axis, about 23.5 degrees from the perpendicular to its orbit, not by changing distance from the Sun. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during Northern Hemisphere winter.

As Earth orbits the Sun, the axis keeps pointing toward the same direction in space. For half the year the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun. Sunlight then strikes it more directly and days are longer, so it receives more energy per square metre: summer. Six months later the Northern Hemisphere tilts away, sunlight arrives at a lower angle and is spread over more area, and days are shorter: winter. The Southern Hemisphere has opposite seasons at the same time.

The solstices, around June 21 and December 21, are the extremes of tilt. The equinoxes, around March 20 and September 22, occur when neither hemisphere tilts toward the Sun and day and night are nearly equal everywhere.
